**Thesis (lean):** Outgrowing your shell means a season of being soft and exposed before you're strong again — and that vulnerable phase isn't the failure, it's the precondition you can't fast-track.

**Proposed shape:**
1. The impasse, named first and personal — open at the specific edge *you* are at (the shell that no longer fits), so by the first block the reader knows this is a real person at a real edge, not a fable.
2. The lobster as the lens for that situation — grows all its life, shell doesn't, so it must go soft and exposed to grow. The metaphor earns its place because it's explaining *your* impasse, not floating free of one.
3. The turn — weakest *is* the moment of greatest potential; the exposed phase is the precondition, not the detour, and it can't be rushed.
4. Positional close — land on a claim about where *you* are or what *you're* choosing in your own shell, not "take your time" advice issued to everyone.

---

**Diagnosis:**

1. **Not original** — the whole spine (growth requires a vulnerable phase → the lobster sheds its shell → don't rush) is one of the most circulated metaphors there is (the rabbi-and-the-lobster parable, the viral stress-makes-you-grow clip). As written, the piece could be any of a hundred lobster posts; the *idea* isn't yours, so the only thing that can make it yours is a specific stake the draft never puts on the page.

2. **Voice absent (clinical / universal-parable)** — there is no "I" anywhere and not one signature move (no self-Q&A, no strikethrough undersell, no pastiche, no specific noticing); a reader who knows *Future Isn't Just Compute* would not recognise you by P2. Passes the hedge budget but reads as a competent devotional anyone could have written — under the 1A rule that is a fail, not a pass.

3. **Buried thesis** — the first four blocks are pure lobster biology; the claim that this is about *us* doesn't arrive until P5 ("a common impasse each of us will face"), and the actual call lands only in the last line. The reader waits four paragraphs to learn what's being claimed about their life, and by blank-line count the thesis is outside the first three blocks.

---

**Working:** "both at its weakest but also at its moment of greatest potential" is the real hinge of the piece — that paradox is the one line doing thinking the metaphor alone doesn't.

---

*Footnote (not a structural issue, flagged per request): the "they are immortal" hook is an overclaim. Lobsters have negligible senescence — they don't weaken with age the way we do — but they do die, and most die *from molting*: each moult gets more energetically costly until one fails, or shell disease sets in. A reader who knows this gets a credibility ding on your very first line, and the metaphor doesn't need it — "grows all its life" already carries the weight, and "the moult eventually kills them" is arguably *better* for your thesis than immortality.*
