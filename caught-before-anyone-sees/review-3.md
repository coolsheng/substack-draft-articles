**Thesis (lean):** The value of public failure was never the lesson — it was the shared vocabulary teammates built around it, and AI-mediated private learning erases that quietly while feeling like progress.

**Proposed shape:**
1. The prod DB story, concrete and specific — land on "I wasn't teaching them the fix, I was teaching them how to talk about the fix."
2. The shorthand persists — a year later the names and story were still load-bearing at 2am.
3. What AI changes — everyone patches privately; correct answers, no shared reference, no common language.
4. The turn — the lesson was always the excuse. What we lose isn't speed of learning; it's the company we used to build around it.

---

**Diagnosis:**

1. **Buried thesis** — the actual claim ("the thing being lost wasn't in the public execution. It was in the language we built around the lesson") doesn't arrive until section 3. The opening section ("We Can Become Experts Overnight") is AI-discourse warmup that ends on a weak rhetorical question ("are we hollow experts?") instead of stating the argument. Cut section 1; lead with the Friday story.
2. **Loop** — sections 2, 3, and 4 all restate the same point three times: "teaching them how to talk about the fix" → "the language we built around the lesson" → "it's the company." Each restatement is more abstract than the last and adds no new evidence. The closing aphorism (destination/journey/company) is a vibe-close substituting for a payoff. Pick one phrasing and earn the turn with a new beat — what *specifically* gets lost when the language fragments.
3. **Not salient** — "Intelligence is knowing fire is hot. Wisdom is knowing not to touch it." The piece isn't about wisdom vs. intelligence; it's about shared vocabulary. The aphorism is a tangent that points the reader at a different argument than the one you're making. Cut it. Same problem with the "Standing on the Shoulder of Giants" title — shoulders-of-giants is about predecessors, but your argument is about contemporaries.

---

**Working:** "I wasn't just teaching them the fix, I was teaching them how to talk about the fix" is the spine of the piece — that one sentence does more work than the surrounding three sections combined. And "correct, but is it right?" is the sharpest line in the draft; it deserves more weight than a parenthetical.
